# The Cialdini Bible — Fleet Persuasion Canon

*The fleet's durable reference for ethical persuasion on every page, email, and
offer we ship. Anchored to Robert Cialdini's own published work — verified from
primary sources held in the fleet's files — plus the conversion evidence base.
This is the deep reference behind the checkable rules in `COPY_STANDARDS.md`
(C-C1…C-C7). The design gate loads this file whenever persuasion patterns are
in play.*

**Source situation (corrected 2026-09-18).** The fleet holds three primary
Cialdini sources in `source-papers/`, each read and verified by a
fleet agent on 2026-09-18. Every experiment cited below traces to one of them.
(They are the founder's papers, kept in the source-papers directory — *not*

## 0. The ethical core — detective, never smuggler

Cialdini's own distinction, in his own words (S1): the **detective** surfaces
influence principles that are *naturally present* in the offer; the
**smuggler** fabricates or counterfeits them. His example: an agent who cites
genuine expertise behind a pain reliever is "doing us a favor" by saving us
the research — but the agency that finds "no particular scientific merit" and
smuggles authority in via "ads featuring actors wearing lab coats" is
manipulating. S2's conclusion (ch. 8) is even blunter: compliance
professionals "who play fairly by the rules of shortcut responding are not to
be considered the enemy; to the contrary, they are our allies… The proper
targets for counter-aggression are only those individuals who falsify,
counterfeit, or misrepresent the evidence that naturally cues our shortcut
responses."

Fleet standing rules that enforce this:
- **Social proof must be REAL.** Never invent testimonials, client logos,
  counts, reviews, or ratings. (FTC Consumer Review Rule, effective Oct 2024:
  fake reviews, review gating, and suppression of negatives carry civil
  penalties — approximately $53,000 per violation at current levels; Fashion
  Nova was fined $4.2M for suppressing negatives.)
- **Sell the premium on what the buyer gains, never on what they should
  dread.** No fear anchoring in marketing (founder's standing rule). Loss
  framing appears in Cialdini's evidence (S1, S2) — the fleet's honest form is
  *foregone gain* ("what they lose by not acting"), never threat or dread.
- **Only real scarcity.** Every scarcity claim is falsifiable — a date, a
  seat count. Manufactured urgency is banned.
- **Every number traces to a checkable source.** No invented figures, ever.

*The smuggler test (run per page element): "If the buyer audited this claim
tomorrow, would it survive?" If not, it doesn't ship.*

## 1. The seven principles — page patterns

### P1 · Reciprocity — give before you ask

**The science (S1, S2).** The norm of reciprocation compels people to return
favors — even small, even unsolicited ones. Disabled American Veterans
solicitations went from **18% to 35% response** when a free unsolicited gift
(address labels) was included (S1). Making a *concession* is also a gift: in
the "zoo trip" study, only **17%** agreed to supervise a zoo trip outright —
but after refusing the experimenter's extreme request (two years of
supervision), **50%** agreed to the retreated-to request. S2: "favors are to
be met with favors" — and the defense is that the rule "does not require
that tricks be met with favors."

**Where it lives.** Above and before the first CTA — the top third of the
page.

**What it looks like.**
- The useful artifact: a checklist, template, audit, teardown, or dataset the
  buyer keeps regardless of whether they buy. (House precedent: playbook excerpts; the services page's cutover-weekend runbook.)
- The free diagnostic: "we'll score your current setup" — the intake's
  qualification step framed as something the *buyer* receives.
- **Honest rejection-then-retreat:** lead with the full engagement; when the
  buyer hesitates, retreat to a genuine smaller offer (the readiness audit,
  the diagnostic) as the real concession — not a fake anchor price. The
  retreated request must be a real offer you'd happily deliver.

**Copy patterns.**
- "Here's the checklist our crew runs on every cutover weekend — yours to
  keep, whether or not we ever work together."
- "Take the 2-minute self-audit. You'll know exactly where you stand."

**Honest check.** The gift must be genuinely useful on its own — not a
brochure with a bow on it. If removing your branding would make it worthless,
it's not reciprocity, it's a flyer.

---

### P2 · Commitment & Consistency — small yes before big yes

**The science (S1, S2).** Once people take a stand — especially actively,
publicly, or in writing — they act to stay consistent with it. In S1's
restaurant study, no-show rates fell from **30% to 10%** when the hostess
changed "Please call if you have to change plans" to **"Will you please
call if you have to change plans?"** — then *paused* and waited for the
caller's verbal "yes." The commitment was active (spoken aloud) and public.
Schwarzwald (1977) found a small petition signature **doubled** later
donation rates to the same cause. S2's mechanism: "Commitment is the Key" —
active, public, effortful commitments reshape the *self-image* ("I'm the
kind of person who…"), and "there is something magical about writing things
down." S2's defenses add two fleet rules: commitments work best when the
choice feels *inner* (freely made, not coerced) — and heavy bribery for a
commitment poisons it (never bribe for testimonials or reviews).

**Where it lives.** The ask architecture — CTAs, forms, and the path between
sections.

**What it looks like — the commitment ladder.**
1. **Micro-yes:** choose a line, answer one question, start the audit. Zero
   identity cost. (House precedent: the intake's `?vertical=` preselect —
   the buyer has already "chosen their line" before the form loads.)
2. **Declared yes:** the form itself — the buyer names the need *in their own
   words* (S2's "magical" written commitment).
3. **Public yes:** the consult call, the booked walkthrough — like the
   restaurant's spoken "yes," it works because they said it aloud.
4. Never ask for the biggest commitment first. Every rung must feel like the
   natural next step from the last.

**Copy patterns.**
- "Choose your line on the form." (not "tell us everything")
- Multi-step forms beat monoliths: one easy question first, details later —
  once someone has started, finishing feels consistent.
- Pause and let them say yes. The restaurant's pause is a page pattern: a
  beat after the question, not a demand.

**Honest check.** Each rung must deliver real value at that rung. A ladder
whose early rungs are empty is a funnel trap, not a commitment device.

---

### P3 · Social proof — similar others, specifically

**The science (S1, S2, S3).** Under uncertainty, people look to others'
behavior to guide their own — most powerfully to *similar* others in the
*same* situation (S2: "especially when we view those others to be similar to
ourselves"). S1's street experiment: a single upward gazer drew **4%** of
passersby to stop; **five** drew **18%**; **fifteen** drew **40%**. But S1
also documents the **boomerang effect**: campaigns publicizing widespread
undesirable behavior backfire — Shaffer (1990) found publicized suicides
increased subsequent suicides, especially among similar others. Cialdini's
warning, verbatim: *"Within the statement 'Look at all the people who are
doing this undesirable thing' lurks the powerful and undercutting message
'Look at all the people who ARE doing this undesirable thing.'"* (S1.) The
lab evidence in S3 confirms it experimentally: a minority norm *alone* pushed
behavior the wrong way (p = .06 backfire, Experiment 1).

**The honest substitute for weak proof — trending norms (S3).** When only a
minority performs the desirable behavior, don't fake a majority. Show the
*trend*: Mortensen et al. found that adding "this increased from 37% in [two
years prior]" to a 48% minority norm beat the minority norm alone *and* beat
no-norm control — internal meta-analysis across 8 studies, **g = 0.29 vs.
norm-only (p = .001), g = 0.14 vs. control (p = .012)**. The mechanism: people
predict the increase will continue (Western audiences; Easterners assume less
trend continuation — S3 cites Alter & Kwan 2009; Ji, Nisbett & Su 2001).
Strikingly, the effect transferred to *related behaviors that were never
mentioned* in the norm message. Page application: publish the real trajectory
("more crews booked readiness audits this quarter than last"), never an
invented majority.

**Practitioner benchmarks (Sept 2026 fleet recon — secondary sources, treat
as rough, not Cialdini findings):** user reviews lift e-commerce conversion
roughly +18–28% (Floyd et al., *Journal of Marketing*, 2014); five reviews
substantially beat zero with diminishing returns after; the 4.0–4.7 star
range converts best — perfect 5.0 triggers skepticism.

**Where it lives — placement cadence.**
| Page zone | Proof type |
|---|---|
| Trust bar, immediately below hero | Logos (recognizable only) + one key metric |
| After problem statement, before solution | First testimonial — validates the pain |
| After services/offer section | Case study with specific numbers |
| Near final CTA | Video testimonial (identified person) |
| Beside the form / at decision friction | Review aggregate or peer quote |

**The credibility anatomy** (every testimonial needs all of these):
full name → job title → company → photo → specific metric or outcome →
direct quote (never paraphrased). 3–5 testimonials is optimal; more than 7
creates cognitive overload. Two detailed case studies outperform eight short
quotes.

**Copy patterns.**
- Named case study: "Reduced processing time 67% in 8 weeks — [Name, Title,
  Company]."
- Trending-norm pattern: "X of Y operators in [peer group] now [do the
  desirable thing] — up from Z last year."

**Honest checks.**
- Logos of unknown companies *reduce* credibility. Anonymous praise is
  worthless — attribute, date, and make checkable, or cut it.
- **Boomerang guard:** never publicize the bad norm. "Most providers ghost
  you after cutover" teaches the buyer that ghosting is normal. Frame the
  *desired* norm: what good buyers do, what good crews do.
- S2's defense made fleet doctrine: counterfeit social evidence deserves
  "belligerent" counter-action — "purchase NO products featured in phony
  'unrehearsed interview' commercials." The detective never stages the
  crowd, never scripts the "interview."
- Never suppress or gate negatives. The FTC rule bans it; the detective
  welcomes the audit.

### P4 · Authority — cite the real credentialing body

**The science (S1, S2).** People defer to legitimate expertise — titles,
credentials, and third-party validation act as decision shortcuts under
complexity. S1's street experiment: a man in a business suit got passersby to
pick up dropped coins or jaywalk at **+350% compliance** over casual dress.
The Robert Young / Marcus Welby effect: an actor in a lab coat sold decaf for
decades. S1's two defense questions — flip them into the fleet's honesty
checks: (1) *Is this authority truly an expert?* (2) *How truthful can we
expect the expert to be?* S2 sharpens the danger: "Connotation not content"
— the *mere appearance* of authority is often enough, which is exactly why
the burden on us is heavier. And S1's sincerity signal: authority gains trust
fastest by **arguing against its own self-interest** — the honest expert who
names the limits of their offer.

**Where it lives.** Beside forms and claims: badge clusters near the email
input, credentials adjacent to the assertions they back, founder authority in
the hero-adjacent zone.

**What it looks like.**
- Third-party badges that actually mean something (G2, Clutch, filed
  certifications) — placed at decision friction points, not dumped in a
  footer.
- Founder authority: real credentials, real history, first-person voice.
  (House precedent: the William biography pages; the founder's signature
  discipline.)
- Cited sources for every substantive claim — the paper, the filing, the
  on-chain record.
- The against-self-interest beat: name what you *can't* do, what you *won't*
  sell. The smuggler never admits limits; the detective leads with them.

**Honest check.** Authority claims name the credentialing body. The services
war room killed "vetted field partners" and implied certifications before
ship — that is this principle working correctly. Borrowed authority ("as seen
in" without the seeing), stock-photo lab coats, and implied credentials are
smuggling — the lab-coat ad Cialdini himself called manipulation (S1).

### P5 · Liking — warmth is real

**The science (S1, S2).** We comply more readily with people we like —
driven by similarity, genuine praise, familiarity, and warmth. Tupperware
sells one party every **2.7 seconds** through friendship, not product (S1).
Aune & Basil (1994): a charity solicitor who said **"I'm a student, too"**
**nearly doubled donations** — similarity, one honest sentence (S1).
Reingen & Kernan (1977): an attractive charity worker drew **42%** of
solicitees to give vs. **23%** for an ordinary one (S1). Cooperation also
builds liking: Good Cop/Bad Cop works because the "good" cop cooperates —
and S2's jigsaw-classroom evidence shows cooperation dissolves even hostile
divides (S2). S2's four drivers: **attractiveness, similarity, compliments,
contact + cooperation** — plus conditioning/association (MasterCard's
 insignia alone increased United Way donations; "known by the company we
kept").

**Where it lives.** Voice and faces: founder first-person where it belongs
(video scripts especially), real team photos, persona-mirrored copy.

**What it looks like.**
- The founder's voice, un-corporated. (Fleet rule C-C5: the voice sounds like
  a person, not a brand deck.)
- Personas the buyer recognizes as *themselves* — the services page's seven
  buyer personas are a liking device: "people like me buy this." That is the
  "I'm a student, too" pattern, deployed honestly.
- Real faces, real names. No stock-team photos. No chatbot named "Sarah."

**Honest check.** Liking is the most counterfeited principle online. S1's
warning: "we're all more guilty of excessive praise than we might care to
admit" — and S1 shows compliments work *even when untrue*, which is why the
fleet never uses them as a lever. If the warmth isn't real, it isn't liking —
it's costume.

### P6 · Scarcity — real constraints only

**The science (S1, S2).** Opportunities seem more valuable when genuinely
limited. S1: after a fire destroyed a Florida State cafeteria, students rated
the food **significantly better once it was unavailable**. Knishinsky's beef
buyers **doubled their orders** on news of scarcity — and when told the
information was *exclusive*, ordered **600% more**: the "scarcity double
whammy" (scarcity + loss of freedom). S2's epigraph (Chesterton): "Less is
Best, and Loss is Worst" — "people are more motivated by the thought of
losing something than by the thought of gaining something of equal value"
(the breast self-exam pamphlet studies). Two structural notes: *New Scarcity*
hurls harder than constant scarcity — a drop from abundance to scarcity
stings more than never-having (reactance: the Romeo & Juliet effect) — and
*competition for scarce resources* creates a "feeding frenzy": S1's explicit
caution — "we should keep our heads and exercise caution," because frenzy
"fuels more irrational behavior." **The fleet therefore never pairs scarcity
with competition, and never manufactures either.**

**Where it lives.** Capacity and calendar — never the offer itself.

**What it looks like.**
- Real capacity: "We run N cutover weekends a quarter" — only if true and
  falsifiable.
- Real deadlines: the federal opportunity calendar, cohort dates, lease
  expiries the buyer already faces.
- **Foregone-gain framing** (the honest form of loss framing): what the
  buyer's *Monday morning* costs them without the fix — grounded in their
  world, never invented countdown timers. The loss is of something they
  already want, never a threat of something they dread.

**Honest check (C-C6).** Every scarcity claim is falsifiable — a date, a
seat count. Countdown widgets, "only 3 left" stock theater, expiring
discounts that never expire, and manufactured feeding frenzies are banned.
When in doubt, cut it: false scarcity is the fastest way to convert a buyer
into a former buyer.

### P7 · Unity — "we is the shared me"

**The science.** The seventh principle (*Pre-Suasion*, ch. 12; elevated in
*Influence*, new and expanded, 2021): shared identity is the strongest lever.
People act for the in-group — family, tribe, crew, lineage — with far less
deliberation than any other principle requires. Insider language,
co-creation, and "we-ness" move behavior. (S1's cross-cultural coda applies
here too: Morris, Podolny & Ariel found the *strongest* lever varies by
audience — the US weights reciprocity, China authority and connections,
Spain liking and friendship, Germany consistency with rules. Know your
buyer; weight the principles accordingly.)

**Where it lives.** The page's identity layer: hero-adjacent framing, section
language, the CTA itself.

**What it looks like.**
- Insider language: the buyer's tribe vocabulary, adopted and amplified —
  "cutover weekend," "the second ticket," "Monday morning."
- The crew frame: "One crew" (fleet precedent: the services page's "One crew"
  section *is* a unity device — a named, accountable crew vs. the anonymous
  contractor marketplace).
- Co-creation: "built with operators like you," the buyer as member rather
  than target.

**Honest check.** Unity curdles into manipulation fastest of all seven —
manufactured tribe ("join the family" from a stranger) reads as cult
recruiting. The shared identity must be *true*: the crew is real, the lineage
is real, the buyer's membership in the story is earned by the work, not
declared by the copy.

## 2. Pre-suasion — the moment before the ask

From *Pre-Suasion*: **what's focal is causal.** What the buyer attends to
just *before* the request shapes what they do next — often more than the
argument itself.

**Page applications.**
- **Attention first.** The hero doesn't argue; it primes. Start with the
  buyer's most acute pain ("Monday morning") — what they attend to first
  colors everything after.
- **Credibility before content.** The most relevant credential in the first
  screenful, not buried.
- **Unity before ask.** Establish the shared identity ("operators like you")
  *before* the CTA appears, so the click feels like joining, not buying.
- **Positive self-framing.** "Are you the kind of operator who…?" — a "yes"
  becomes a commitment the rest of the page holds them to (P2).

**Cialdini's own demo close (S1).** He ends the *Scientific American* article
by openly deploying all six principles on the reader — and annotating them:
rejection-then-retreat ("read both books; if you can't do that, would you
show it to just one friend?"), reciprocity ("I'm going to throw in… a set of
references"), social validation ("many others just like you will certainly
consent"), liking ("I genuinely like you… I love that shirt"), commitment
("I am pausing politely here" — the restaurant pause), and the authority of
the whole article. It is the canonical honest conversion close: every device
visible, every claim true. The fleet's CTAs should survive that same
annotation.

## 3. The buying queue — sequencing a stranger to yes

The order matters as much as the elements. Six stations:

1. **Orient** — hero: name the pain, mirror the identity. (Pre-suasion,
   Unity, Liking)
2. **Agitate honestly** — the cost of the status quo, in the buyer's own
   terms. No fear anchoring: the premium is on what they gain — or, at most,
   the foregone gain of inaction. (House precedent: "The second ticket.")
3. **Differentiate** — why the alternatives fail, with mechanism not
   adjectives. (House precedent: the anonymous-contractor-marketplace
   finding.) (Authority, P-C4)
4. **Prove** — mechanism made visible; honest substitutes where proof is
   still being earned (§5). Trending norms where majorities don't exist yet
   (S3). (Social proof, Authority)
5. **De-risk** — the guarantee / process promise, placed exactly at the
   anxiety point: under the CTA, beside the form. (Risk reversal)
6. **Ladder the ask** — small yes → big yes; never the biggest commitment
   first. Multiple doors for multiple readiness levels. (Commitment,
   Reciprocity)

*Rule: proof adjacent to the claim it validates; the guarantee adjacent to
the button it de-risks; the ask never before the value.*

## 4. Risk reversal — carry the risk yourself

Hormozi's thesis (*$100M Offers*): the biggest objection to any sale is the
risk it won't work — and reversing risk is the number-one conversion lever
(practitioner reports of 2–4x lifts from guarantee quality alone; the math
nets positive even at doubled refund rates).

**For services, guarantee the process, not the outcome you can't un-deliver:**
- "Rework it until it meets the runbook."
- Response-time commitments ("we respond within one business day").
- The named-crew promise: accountability as the guarantee.
- Place it where anxiety peaks: under the CTA, beside the form, in the
  confirmation message (kills buyer's remorse post-submit).

**Honest check.** A guarantee is a promise you must be able to keep at scale.
Never guarantee what operations can't deliver — the guarantee is operations'
signature, not marketing's.

## 5. Honest substitutes — the zero-proof playbook

When you have no testimonials yet (the fleet's real position on new pages),
you do not invent proof. You substitute:

1. **Process transparency.** Show the runbook, the mechanism, the checklist.
   Specificity is credible even without a testimonial (P-C4: show the
   mechanism).
2. **Trending norms (S3).** Don't fake a majority — show the real trajectory.
   "48% of [peer group] now do X — up from 37% two years ago" is Cialdini's
   own answer to the no-majority problem.
3. **Risk reversal.** Carry the risk yourself (§4). The guarantee *is* the
   testimonial you haven't earned yet.
4. **Commitment ladders.** Let them start small — a vertical choice, a
   question, an audit. Every completed rung is self-generated proof.
5. **Verifiable facts.** Every number traces to a source (N-2). A sourced
   fact beats an anonymous quote.
6. **Founder voice.** A real person stands behind it — name, face, signature
   discipline. (Liking, Authority)
7. **The work diary.** "Here's what we're building now." Proof compounds;
   publish the building.
8. **Specificity as proof.** Concrete detail ("the 11pm DNS cutover, the
   rollback plan in the van") is believable in a way adjectives never are.

*The rule: absence of proof is a temporary condition with a plan, never a
license to fabricate.*

## 6. The persuasion audit — gate-ready checks

Score any page, email, or offer 1–5 per principle. Ship threshold: **3+ on
Social proof (or its honest substitute), Authority, and Liking**; target all
seven present, average 4+.

| # | Check | Fail looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reciprocity: the page gives real value before the first CTA | CTA before any useful content |
| 2 | Commitment: asks ladder small→big; no biggest-first ask | "Book a $50k engagement" as the only door |
| 3 | Social proof: real, attributed, specific, well-placed — or honest substitutes deployed | Anonymous quotes, invented counts, proof dumped at bottom |
| 4 | Authority: claims name their credentialing body/source | "Certified experts" with no certifier |
| 5 | Liking: a real human voice is audible | Brand-deck copy, stock faces |
| 6 | Scarcity: every constraint is real and falsifiable | Countdown timers, "only 3 left" theater |
| 7 | Unity: a true shared identity, primed before the ask | "Join the family" from a stranger |
| 8 | Pre-suasion: hero primes the pain/identity before any argument | Hero argues features |
| 9 | Queue order: orient → agitate → differentiate → prove → de-risk → ladder | Guarantee before value; ask before proof |
| 10 | Smuggler test: every claim survives a buyer audit tomorrow | Any claim you'd flinch at if screenshotted |

## 7. The banned list

Never on a fleet property, no exceptions:
- Invented testimonials, client logos, review counts, ratings, or case studies.
- Review gating or suppression of negatives (FTC rule).
- Fake scarcity: countdown timers, phantom stock, expiring discounts that
  don't expire; manufactured feeding frenzies.
- Fear anchoring: selling on dread rather than gain. (Foregone-gain framing
  is the honest limit.)
- Anonymous praise presented as proof.
- Perfect-5.0-rating theater.
- Publicizing the bad norm (boomerang) — "everyone else is doing it wrong."
- Borrowed authority — "as featured in" without the feature; lab-coat
  theater.
- The biggest ask as the only door.
- Bribed commitments — reviews or testimonials bought with discounts or
  gifts (S2: heavy bribery poisons the commitment).
