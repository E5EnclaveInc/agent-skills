# Media Company Marketing

## Purpose
Run a marketing task like a media company that happens to sell a product (or a cause): win attention with entertainment, convert it into connection and trust over repeated touchpoints — never lead with sales messaging.

## Workflow
1. Pre-commit the metric. Before any asset is made, write one line: why we are creating this, what business goal it serves, and how success is measured. Default north-star metrics: quality comments and shares. Without this, "you're a paper bag in the wind."
2. Name the passenger. Define the one specific audience the piece is for — and make it instantly obvious who it's for in the first words, caption, tags, or location. Get granular; speak their language; be where they actually talk (their communities and groups, not yours). Check analytics demographics afterward; if the data contradicts the target, pivot.
3. Choose entertainment, not features. People are allergic to sales messaging and being told what to do; they come to social for relief and entertainment. Build episodic, addictive, relatable series that people come back for — not product tours. (Case file in `references/case-file.md`.)
4. Place it on the funnel. Every piece has one job: **ATTENTION** (first contact, always the first job) → **CONNECTION** (it resonates, it's seen repeatedly, it earns a follow) → **TRUST** (conversion). Discoverability is everywhere — a clip, an algorithm, a share in a group chat — so plan multiple touchpoints of discoverability rather than one hero post.
5. Pick the personal-brand lane. A personal brand fast-tracks trust because people trust people, not logos — but not every founder needs one. If the founder resists: deputize employees to tell the brand's stories, or if no people are available, commit to one great repeatable brand story told well.
6. Choose depth deliberately. Memorable minutes beat forgettable seconds: long-form can carry attention → trust in a single asset; short-form needs weeks or months of touchpoints to complete the same journey. Match length to how far along the funnel the piece must carry the viewer.
7. Judge virality correctly. A viral spike is a sugar rush — buzz, then the comedown. It means you appeared in front of many people, not that you won an audience. The goal is repeated exposure to the *right* people, building a relationship over time. A viral post that never reaches the right passenger is a miss against the pre-committed metric.
8. Run consistency × experimentation. Consistency alone plateaus. Ship on cadence, run a weekly review of what worked AND what didn't, lean into new platform features early, and leave room to ride a trend — never let a batched monthly calendar block a timely response.
9. Diagnose against the pre-set metric. Evaluate every post against the metric committed in step 1, even when surface numbers look good.

## Output Contract
A content brief per initiative: goal, pre-committed metric, audience (the passenger), entertainment format, funnel job (attention / connection / trust), personal-brand lane choice, depth choice, cadence + weekly review loop, and a post-ship diagnosis against the pre-set metric.

## Operating Rules
1. No sales-first content. If the asset's first job is selling, rewrite it until its first job is entertaining.
2. Metric before making. Nothing ships without a pre-committed metric line.
3. Speak to someone or no one. If you can't name the passenger in one sentence, the piece isn't ready.
4. Relationship over reach. Virality is a sugar rush; repeated exposure to the right people wins.
5. Brand-agnostic execution. The same doctrine applies to a product business and a nonprofit campaign; adapt the format, never the principles.
6. Data beats ego. When analytics contradict the target audience, pivot the content.
